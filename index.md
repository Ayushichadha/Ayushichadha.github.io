<section class="intro"><h1>Ayushi Chadha</h1><p class="lead">I’m interested in how intelligence works and in building systems that can reason, learn, and extend what people can do.</p>
<p>My independent research focuses on <strong>latent and recurrent reasoning</strong>: how models work through a problem by repeatedly updating their internal state. I’m drawn to the possibility that better reasoning can come from how this computation is organized and controlled. The conversation around GPT-6 Astra has been exciting to follow through that lens; these are questions I’ve been pursuing since 2025.</p>
<p>I’ve explored one part of this design space across two sole-author papers. In my first paper, I investigated how long an internal subgoal should persist before being revised. The follow-up takes the next step: can that decision be learned from the model’s internal state, and does learning it actually improve the outcome? This has led me toward broader questions about credit assignment, adaptive computation, and the evaluation of systems that supervise other processes.</p>
<p>Before pursuing independent research, I led the design, implementation, and testing of a domain-grounded LLM system at Propero Consulting. I proposed ShopiBot and developed its retrieval and query-handling pipelines for Shopify developers, moving between research papers, experiments, and product requirements. My responsibilities also included internal technical seminars, demonstrations for prospective partners, and working with the CEO on technical direction and product decisions.</p>
<p>My background is in electrical engineering, and my curiosity often takes me into cognition and neuroscience. I’m especially interested in reasoning systems and AI that can extend human attention, memory, and imagination. Reading, building, and testing ideas are how I keep working toward that larger ambition.</p>

<article class="paper"><h3>Beyond the Clock: Measuring the Value of Adaptive Revision</h3><p class="meta"><span class="status">Under review</span> · Sole author · 2026</p><p>Does adapting a revision decision improve the final answer? A controlled study of a hierarchical reasoner separates state-dependent scores, changed behavior, and outcome value through frozen-checkpoint interventions and timing comparisons. The caution extends to evaluating compound agentic systems: a supervisor changing prompts, programs, or agent harnesses is not by itself evidence of better outcomes. The paper proposes auditing those decisions against strong fixed rules and controlled interventions; this broader application remains to be tested.</p><div class="links"><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00874">Paper</a></div></article></section>
